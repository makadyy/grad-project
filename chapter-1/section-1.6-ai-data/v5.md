## 1.6 Data Science and Artificial Intelligence Components

LabyrinthAI uses data for two opposite purposes:

- **Real data teaches the system.** Recorded network traffic, already marked as normal or
  attack, trains the classifier to tell legitimate users from attackers, and novices from
  skilled attackers.
- **Fake data deceives the attacker.** Generated emails, documents, and records fill the
  Digital Twin so it looks like the real network.

A third source appears once the Digital Twin — an isolated replica of the corporate
network, built from fake servers and files, into which attackers are redirected instead of
reaching the real ones — is running. Every attacker who enters is recorded: the commands
they run, the files they open, the time between their actions. One attacker's visit
produces one **session log**. These logs are the project's own data, collected from its own
system, and are used to improve the classifier beyond what a public dataset from 2018 can
teach it.

### 1.6.1 Dataset Description and Source

| Dataset | Source / Owner | Size / Records | Features / Data Type | Target / Labels | License / Access |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CSE-CIC-IDS 2018 | CSE and the Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity, University of New Brunswick | ≈16M connection records, 10 days; ≈6.5 GB CSV | 80 measurements per connection, extracted by CICFlowMeter-V3; raw packet captures; host logs | `Label`: Benign, or one of 7 attack types | Free for academic use; citation required |
| Digital Twin session logs | Collected by the project through Wazuh/ELK | Grows from Phase 2 onward | Commands run, files opened, timing, movement between machines | Attacker skill level, set by the project's own attack team | Project-owned; isolated lab |
| Decoy corpus | Generated by the project using Llama 3 (via Ollama) and Faker | Generated on demand | Emails, reports, employee and finance tables | None — this is output, not training input | Project-owned; fully invented |

**Why this dataset.** KDD Cup 99 and NSL-KDD describe networks that no longer exist.
DARPA 98/99 was generated artificially rather than captured. CSE-CIC-IDS 2018 was recorded
from a real victim organisation of five departments, 420 client machines, and 30 servers,
attacked by 50 machines running tools still in use. Its attack types — brute force, DoS,
DDoS, web attack, infiltration, botnet — are the same behaviours LabyrinthAI must
recognise.

**Known limitations.** The dataset was checked, not assumed. Four flaws shape the cleaning
steps in Section 1.6.3:

- **Class imbalance.** Most records are normal traffic. Some attacks appear only tens of
  times. A model can therefore score highly while catching nothing.
- **Label quality.** Engelen et al. (2021) found records marked with the wrong attack type,
  mostly in the Infiltration class. Each attack type is checked against the schedule the
  authors published; any type that cannot be trusted is excluded, and the exclusion stated.
- **File defects.** Column headings repeat in the middle of some files. Some records are
  exact copies of others. Two speed columns, `Flow Bytes/s` and `Flow Packets/s`, contain
  `Infinity` and `NaN` where a connection lasted no measurable time.
- **Environment specificity.** Addresses, port numbers, and timestamps describe the one
  network that was recorded, not how attackers behave. Section 1.6.3 removes them.

### 1.6.2 Data Collection Method

| Collection Aspect | Planned Method / Decision |
|---|---|
| **Source and acquisition** | • **Public dataset** — download the ready-made CSV files from CIC. Keeps the measurements identical to the studies compared in Chapter 2.<br>• **Session logs** — collected by Wazuh/ELK from every fake service in the Digital Twin.<br>• **Fake content** — generated on project machines. No outside service is called. |
| **Sampling / coverage** | • All ten recording days are kept, so every attack type is present.<br>• Normal traffic is reduced only in the training portion, by the step in Section 1.6.3. |
| **Labeling / annotation** | • **Public dataset** — labels come from the schedule the authors followed when running their attacks. Checked before use.<br>• **Session logs** — labelled by the Red Team, the project members who play the attacker. | **Permissions / ethics** | • **Public dataset** — published for academic use, cited. Recorded from a laboratory organisation, so no personal data.<br>• **Our own recordings** — isolated lab, machines the team owns, attacked by the team. Features describing it are removed.<br>• **Operator** — a small Red Team attacks in a recognisable style. Attacks are scripted from standard tools, and the operator is rotated. |

### 1.6.3 Data Preprocessing

Anything the pipeline learns from the data — averages used for scaling, encodings,
resampling — is calculated on the training portion alone, then applied unchanged to the
validation and test portions.

| Stage | Planned Operation | Reason / Leakage Control |
|---|---|---|
| **Data quality checks** | • Remove repeated column headings.<br>• Delete records that are exact copies.<br>• Replace `Infinity` and `NaN` in the two speed columns.<br>• Check record counts against the published schedule. | • Copies are deleted before the split. The same record in training and test makes results look better with nothing learned.<br>• The impossible speed values come from connections of zero length. Left in, they spread through every later calculation. |
| **Cleaning / transformation** | • Remove columns that identify machines rather than behaviour: source and destination IP address, source and destination port, `Flow ID`, `Timestamp`.<br>• Remove columns holding the same value in every record.<br>• Make attack names consistent. | • The most important step in the pipeline.<br>• Keep the attacker's IP and the model learns "traffic from that machine is an attack" instead of what an attack looks like. It scores near-perfectly in testing and fails against any attacker it has not seen.<br>• Keep `Timestamp` and it learns the attack schedule instead of the attacks. |
| **Feature engineering** | • Keep measurements that describe behaviour: how long a connection lasted, packets and bytes each way, gaps between packets, packet sizes, TCP flag counts.<br>• Add two ratios — packets sent versus received, and average bytes per packet. Both describe the shape of an exchange regardless of its size.<br>• Where two measurements always rise and fall together, keep the one easier to explain. | • Behaviour transfers to the Digital Twin. Raw volumes reflect the speed of the recorded network.<br>• Explainability is a requirement, not a preference. The SHAP/LIME layer must show the analyst which measurement caused a decision, and cannot do that with a measurement no human understands. |
| **Scaling / encoding** | • Rescale numerical measurements to a common range, using averages from the training portion only.<br>• Convert attack names to numbers.<br>• Use the tokenizer that ships with Llama 3 for generated text. | • Averages taken across all the data let the test data influence training. Results then look better than the system will perform.<br>• The rescaling values are saved and reused when the system runs live, so live traffic is treated exactly like training traffic. |
| **Imbalance / augmentation** | • Reduce the number of normal records.<br>• Create extra examples of rare attacks using SMOTE.<br>• Give rare attacks more weight during training.<br>• Merge or drop attack types with too few records to learn from, and say which were dropped. | • Applied to the training portion only.<br>• Creating extra examples before splitting puts near-copies of training records into the test set.<br>• Validation and test keep the real mix of normal and attack traffic. The false-alarm rate reported in Section 1.6.6 only means something against realistic proportions. |

**Decoy corpus quality gate.** Generated content is what the system produces, not what it
learns from, so it is checked differently:

- **Internal consistency.** An employee named in a fake email must exist in the fake
  employee list. File paths must lead somewhere. One contradiction tells the attacker the
  environment is a trap.
- **No real data leaked.** Content is checked for personal details the language model may
  have reproduced from its own training data.
- **No credential collision.** No invented password may match a real one used anywhere in
  the project.

## Terms you'll be asked about

- **Digital Twin** — a fake copy of the whole company network, sealed off from the real one.
- **Decoy** — one fake thing inside it. A fake server, a fake file share.
- **Red Team** — your teammates playing the attacker, on purpose, to test the trap.
- **Dataset** — a big table. One row per recorded connection.
- **Record / row** — one connection between two machines.
- **Feature / measurement** — one column. Example: how many packets were sent.
- **Label** — the answer column. Normal, or which attack it was.
- **Classifier** — the model that reads a row and answers "normal or attack, and how skilled."
- **Training** — showing the model rows with answers so it learns the pattern.
- **Split** — cutting the data into three parts.
    - Training: it learns from this.
    - Validation: you tune settings on this.
    - Test: touched once, at the end, for an honest score.
- **Session log** — the recording of one attacker's whole visit to your trap.
- **SMOTE** — makes extra copies of rare attacks so the model sees enough of them.
- **SIEM (Wazuh/ELK)** — the system that collects logs from everywhere into one place.
- **Tokenizer** — chops text into pieces a language model can read.
- **KDD Cup 99** — 1999 benchmark dataset built from 1998 military network traffic. Three
  quarters of it is duplicate records.
- **NSL-KDD** — 2009 cleanup of KDD Cup 99. Duplicates removed, but still 1998 traffic.

## The two ideas you must defend

- **Leakage — the model sees something it won't have in real life.**
    - Leave the attacker's IP in, and it memorises "that machine is bad."
    - Scores 99%. Catches nothing new.
    - So you delete IPs, ports, timestamps.
- **Imbalance — almost every record is normal traffic.**
    - Always answering "normal" scores ~83% and catches zero attacks.
    - So you rebalance training data only.
    - Rebalance the test set and you grade yourself on a fake exam.

Dr. Sondos will ask about one of these two.

## The bigger problem, not yet raised with anyone

CSE-CIC-IDS 2018 may be the wrong dataset for what this system does.

- It holds **network flow** data: packet counts, byte counts, timings.
- The classifier is meant to judge attacker skill from **what they do inside a decoy**:
  commands typed, files opened, how they move.
- Those are different kinds of data. Training on one, deploying on the other, does not
  transfer.

Two honest ways out:

- Accept the classifier is a conventional network intrusion detector, and drop the
  skill-adaptive claim to something smaller.
- Or add command-level data: **Cowrie honeypot logs**, **DARPA OpTC**, or the
  **LANL host/network dataset**.
